Operations & ERP System — bringing sales, labor, and store performance into one place

A 3-location fusion café brand ran on spreadsheets and group chats. Here's how I found the gap, built the system to close it, and used the data to change how the business staffs its branches.

01 · Context

A 3-branch café brand, growing faster than its systems

La Forêt is a fusion café brand with three locations across Metro Vancouver and Burnaby. I joined as Marketing Coordinator / HQ Operations — a role that sits across both brand marketing and day-to-day operations, which meant I was close enough to every branch to see where the business was actually losing time and visibility.

This case study covers the part of that role that maps most directly onto Operations, Business Analyst, and Brand Experience work: identifying an operational gap, designing a system to close it, and using the resulting data to inform real staffing decisions.

02 · Business Problem

No shared visibility across branches

Day-to-day operations — order tracking, staff scheduling, supplier coordination, and performance reporting — relied on fragmented, manual workflows: spreadsheets, group chats, and paper logs kept separately by each branch.

There was no single source of truth. HQ had no real-time way to see labor cost, order status, or performance across branches, which made it difficult to catch inefficiencies early or compare how locations were actually performing against each other.

03 · My Role

I wasn't hired to build a system — I noticed one was missing

My role spans marketing and HQ operations, not software development. But that position meant I saw the same visibility gap from both sides — trying to plan brand initiatives without reliable operational data, and watching branch managers re-enter the same information into three different places.

I proposed and led the internal ERP build myself, end to end: scoping what data actually needed to exist, designing the structure, building it, and running the pilot — alongside my existing marketing and brand responsibilities, not instead of them.

04 · Process

Requirements → build → pilot → fix → roll out

1. Map the gap
Talked to branch managers and HQ to find out what information was missing, duplicated, or too slow to reach the people who needed it.
2. Structure the data
Defined the core modules needed — orders, branches, team, suppliers, sales, labor — and how they should connect to each other.
3. Build & pilot
Built the store-to-HQ ordering system first and piloted it at a single branch before touching the others.
4. Fix & roll out
Used pilot feedback to fix issues (e.g. cart not clearing after an emergency re-send) before rolling out company-wide.
05 · Solution

One internal system instead of five disconnected habits

I designed and built an internal ERP system from the ground up to centralize operational information — giving HQ and branch managers one place to see orders, staffing, suppliers, and performance across all three locations, instead of the previous mix of spreadsheets, group chats, and paper logs.

08 · Result / Impact

From scattered files to one operational system

The ERP replaced fragmented, manual tracking with a single operational system — giving HQ real-time visibility into orders, labor, and performance across all three branches for the first time. Findings from the labor-cost analysis are now informing staffing and scheduling adjustments, and the store-to-HQ ordering module has moved from a single-branch pilot to a company-wide rollout.

Quantified before/after results — labor cost % change, efficiency gains, hours of manual work removed per week — will be added here once the current rollout data is finalized. I'd rather show this case study with real numbers pending than fill the gap with estimates.

09 · What I Learned

The hard part wasn't the system — it was getting three branches to trust it

Most operational problems aren't really technology problems — they're visibility problems. Building the ERP was the more straightforward half. The harder part was change management: getting three branches with different habits to move off familiar spreadsheets and group chats and trust a new shared system instead.

That's the skill I'd bring to an Operations Coordinator, Business Analyst, or Brand Experience role — not just the ability to design a system, but the patience to roll it out, fix what breaks during a pilot, and get a whole team to actually use it.
